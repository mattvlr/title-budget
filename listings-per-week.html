Reselling arithmetic · listing pace

How many items do I need to list a week to make $1,000 a month?

About 10 — and that number barely moves with your sell-through rate. At a steady state you sell what you list, whatever the rate. What the rate decides is how much stock you must own to produce it, and how long the climb from an empty shelf takes. Advice that gives you the first number without the second two is the thing this page is for.

The short answer. At $23.80 of profit a sale you need 43 sales a month, which is 10 listings a week — once you already hold about 430 active listings. Starting from nothing at 10 a week, month 36 is the first one that actually clears $1,000. Listing 14 a week gets you there inside a year.

Where the 43 comes from

Item price $42.00 Shipping the buyer paid $8.00 eBay's fee, 13.6% + $0.40 -$7.20 ------- You receive $42.80 What you paid for the item -$12.00 Postage label -$6.40 Packaging -$0.60 ------- Profit on one sale $23.80 $1,000 / $23.80 = 43 sales a month 43 sales / 4.33 weeks = 10 listings a week

42 sales would leave you $0.40 short, which is why the figure rounds up. Your own profit per sale is the number that matters here and it is almost certainly not $23.80 — put your own price, postage, item cost and label into the free fee comparison and read the profit column, then divide.

The last column is the point: it does not move. At a steady state the number of items you sell in a month equals the number you listed that month, whatever fraction of the shelf is turning over — a slow shelf is simply a bigger one. The stock you must own swings from 144 to 860, a factor of 6, and so does the patience. Sell-through is defined here as items sold divided by items active, over one month. Other people define it over 90 days or against sold plus active; a rate from somewhere else is not necessarily this rate.

The bits most summaries get wrong

  • The listings-a-week number does not depend on your sell-through rate. At a steady state you sell what you list. The rate sets the size of the shelf — 144 listings at the fastest rate here against 860 at the slowest, a factor of 6 — and the length of the climb. Not the pace.
  • Your first month is not a share of the target. At 10 a week it is about $102, because you are selling out of a shelf that is still nearly empty. Anyone promising the target in the first month is selling something.
  • “List ten a day” is a pace, not advice. Ten a day is 303 listings a month, which at $23.80 a sale settles at about $7,219 a month. That is excellent if it is your target and a lot of wasted evenings if $1,000 is.
  • Where you list barely matters to this question. 9 to 11 listings a week across seven marketplaces, against a factor of 6 in the stock you must own from the sell-through rate alone.
  • A target in dollars is not a target in sales. It is a target in dollars divided by profit per sale, and the second number is the one you control. $5 more per item is worth 80 fewer listings on the shelf.

Questions people actually ask

How many items do I need to list a week to make $1,000 a month?

About 10, at $23.80 of profit a sale — but only once you already hold about 430 active listings. 10 listings a week is 43 a month, and at a steady state the number you sell in a month equals the number you listed. Starting from an empty shelf at that pace, month 36 is the first one that actually clears $1,000. Listing 14 a week instead gets you there inside a year.

Does a better sell-through rate mean I can list fewer items?

No. It means you need to own fewer. At a steady state, sales per month equal listings per month whatever the rate, so the listings-per-week figure is 10 either way. What changes is the shelf: 144 active listings at a 30% monthly sell-through against 860 at 5%, a factor of 6.

How many active listings do I need for $1,000 a month?

About 430 at a 10% monthly sell-through and $23.80 of profit a sale: 43 sales a month divided by the rate at which your stock sells. Halve the sell-through and you double the shelf; the table on this page has the others.

Why does it take so long at the right number of listings a week?

Because the inventory has to exist before it can produce. Listing 10 a week from an empty shelf, your first month is 43 listings and about 4 sales — roughly $102, not $1,000. Each month's new listings land on a shelf that is also selling down, so the active count approaches 430 rather than jumping to it, and 36 months pass before a month's own profit reaches the target. The 22 months to 90% of the stock is the honest milestone; the last stretch is the slowest part.

Is “list ten a day” good advice?

It is a pace, not advice, and it is a bigger one than most people asking this question want. Ten a day is 303 listings a month, which at $23.80 of profit a sale settles at about $7,219 a month — excellent if that is the target, and a lot of evenings wasted if $1,000 is.

Does it matter which marketplace I list on?

Barely, for this question. The same $1,000 needs between 9 and 11 listings a week across the seven marketplaces compared here, because the fee difference moves the profit per sale by a few dollars. The sell-through rate moves the stock you have to own by a factor of 6. Which you sell on is a smaller decision than how fast it moves.

Where these numbers come from, and what they are not

The profit per sale is computed from the fee model inside the free fee comparison, parsed out of that page rather than retyped, so the two cannot drift apart. The rest is arithmetic on one recurrence: each month the shelf loses the fraction that sells and gains what you listed. Rates are standard selling fees checked September 2026, excluding promoted-listing ad rates, store subscriptions, international fees and category exceptions. The worked sale is the same $42 item with $8 postage used on the payout page and the offer page, so all three agree.

What this page does not cover

It is arithmetic on a steady rate, not a forecast. It assumes your sell-through and your profit per sale hold still, which nobody's do; it ignores seasonality, price changes, markdowns, items that end unsold and items you give up on. It does not price your time, your storage, or the money tied up in 430 unsold items — which at $12 of cost each is the real reason the slow row is hard, and it is a number only you can put on. It says nothing about what any marketplace's search or recommendation system does with more listings, because that is not knowable from here: more listings give more chances to be found, and this page will not pretend to know the rate. It does not cover sourcing, promoted listings, store subscriptions, taxes, or whether any of this is a good use of your evenings. One thing only: how a monthly profit target turns into a number of listings, and what it actually takes to get there.
